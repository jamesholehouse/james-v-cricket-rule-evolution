The Laws of Cricket, like the English Common Law, are
essentially English in that they have developed according to the
needs of each particular age. Perhaps one of the reasons why this
book is as fascinating as it is informative is because it tells of the
circumstances which forced each change in the Laws of Cricket

Laws of Cricket since the seventeenth century from other
material of doubtful reliability. In the first part of the book he
traces their development from cricket’s earliest days as a game

FOREWORD
by Sir Norman Birkett
Col. Rait Kerr has invited me to write a short Foreword to his
book on the Laws of Cricket, and I am deeply sensible of the
honour he confers on me by so doing. It is customary for a Fore-
word to be written by an acknowledged authority on the subject
matter of the book, who commends the author and his work to the
reading public. Authorities on the Laws of Cricket are not too
easy to find, and if there be such, I am quite sure that I have no
place in their company; but having read this book with unusual
pleasure, I can and do commend the author and his work with
the greatest sincerity. Col. Rait Kerr is the present Secretary of
the M.C.C., and that very great authority, Sir Pelham Warner,
has described him as the ablest Secretary the M.C.C. has ever
possessed, and when paying that high tribute, added the significant
words “and you cannot stump him on the laws of cricket.” This
book makes the truth of chat saying abundantly plain by the remark-
able knowledge displayed on every page. Col. Rait Kerr has written
a most scholarly and authoritative treatise on the Laws of Cricket,
the result of wide reading and careful study over many years, and
it may fairly claim to be a piece of thoughtful and original
research in fields where few dare venture.
Cricket, more than any other game perhaps, has attracted a
great host of writers in every age, and the literature of cricket is
as rich and varied as any to be found in other departments of
our national life. I am familiar with some of it, but Col. Rait
Kerr is familiar with it all. Exercising the most praiseworthy
restraint, he has confined himself to what is strictly relevant to
his immediate theme. For until this book appeared, there was
no book in all the literature of cricket that was devoted solely
to the development of the Laws of Cricket, and certainly no
book that related the growth of the Laws and their formulation
to the background of circumstance out of which the Laws
emerged. Not that literature is absent from these pages, and

INTRODUCTION
Many writers have devoted their life’s work to the study of the
traditions and history of Cricket, with the result that no English
game is more generously endowed with a literature of its own.
The readers of the cricket world play an important part in main-
taining interest in the great traditions of a great game, and it is a
tragedy that so often they find it difficult to acquire either the
classics or the essential historical works of men like William
Denison, Rev. James Pycroft, Arthur Haygarth, F. S. Ashley-
Cooper, P. F. Thomas and G. B. Buckley—to mention but a few.
The excuse for this publication is twofold—firstly that, to the
best of my knowledge, no book dealing solely with the building
up of the Laws of Cricket has been previously attempted, and
secondly that, by shamelessly drawing on the works of the
writers referred to, I am able to provide the setting in which the
legislation of the game developed, and perhaps to induce in my
readers a desire for wider and deeper reading.
In his last published pamphlet P. F. Thomas1 concluded his
erudite historical analysis with the words, “Good luck to anybody
who can add more material, score out the unsuspected inaccura-
cies, and mould the whole together more consistently,” and, to
some degree, it is in answer to his challenge that my contribution
is offered.
Amongst other duties which fall to him, a Secretary of the
Marylebone Cricket Club acts as advisor on points arising from
the Laws of Cricket, not only to the various committees with
which he is associated, but also to the countless cricketers, cricket
lovers and cricket organisations who from time to time seek
enlightment on such matters. On my appointment to the post,
my own knowledge of the Laws was that of the normal club

Chapter One
THE EARLY DAYS OF CRICKET
In any study of the Laws of Cricket there arc two sharply defined
periods—from the earliest days when the game was probably one
for boys, up to about 1750 when the formation of the Hamblcdon
Club led to a great advance in the science of the game, and then
from that date up to the present day. The former period may be
termed the dark ages of Cricket history during which the rules
under which the game was played are largely a matter of surmise,
for, as we shall see, the earliest Laws that have come down to us
are those of about 1744; before that year historians are dependent
on press notices of a somewhat factual type, on a very little
descriptive writing, and on a considerable amount of intelligent
and well-reasoned guess-work.
It has always been accepted that the game grew up as a simple
village pastime in the Weald of Kent, Surrey and Sussex; Thomas
more tlian any other writer has shed light on its development since
the days of 1550 when John Derrick with “diuerse of liis fellowes
did runne and play at Creckett” on a “parcell of land” near the
“Free Schoole of Guideford.” (Extract from the Book of Court
of Guildford for the year 1598.)
Thomas dissected the 1744 Laws, and by discarding words
which would hardly have been in current use amongst the villagers
of the Weald during the seventeenth century evolved a code
which may well have governed the conduct of the village game of
the period. Though no doubt each district had its own variations
on points of detail, the main structure of the game must owe its
establishment to this era.
Various causes delayed the advent of big cricket. In the early
part of the seventeenth century the aristocracy had many alterna-
tive pursuits to serve as an outlet for the gambling spirit of the
time. During their enforced retreat to their country seats in the
Commonwealth days, however, village cricket began to serve as a
medium for playing for stakes, and there is little doubt that this

After the Restoration the Nobility and Gentry of the day
brought cricket back with them to London, where it was estab-
lished at the Artillery Ground in Finsbury Fields, which even at
that time was enclosed by a high brick wall. In October 1689 the
Honourable Artillery Company banned the playing of games on
their property', of which Howe wrote, “This field, and two others
adjoining, had been infectious and very grievous to the city and to
passengers, who by all means endeavoured to shun those fields as
loathsome both to sight and smell.” This however does not seem
to have prevented its use for cricket, and long before 1744 the
ground superintendent, George Smith, was charging sums varying
between twopence and sixpence for admission.
This ground in particular became the scene of the Grand
Matches, which were played for considerable stakes, and which
quite naturally required the drawing up of “Articles of Agree-
ment” to eliminate the risk of much disputing. Buckley has told
us much about these Articles, which he has traced back as far as
1722, and it is clear that they were mainly concerned with the
financial arrangements of a match, but rarely referring to play-
ing conditions except on such points as might require co-ordina-
tion owing to some difference between local custom. Dr. H. F.
Squire, during recent research into early cricket in Sussex, has
found in the archives of the Duke of Richmond and Gordon the

If Goldwin’s description is analysed it will be seen that he has
written of a game which might well have been played under the
1755 Laws—Umpires, notchers, two-stump wicket with bail,
leather ball are all there. The conduct of the game included
tossing for innings, the call of “Play,” the four-ball over, dis-
missals by catching and running out, and perhaps most significant
of all, in two places Goldwin refers to “Laws.” It really appears
that there was very little change in the shape of the game between
1706 and the time when there is a written code of the Laws to
which we can refer, and it is quite likely that Goldwin has in
effect described for us the Wealden game of the seventeenth
century.
Both Lord Harris in his Foreword to Altham’s History of
Cricket, and Thomas have questioned the accuracy of Goldwin’s
observation on one point. In lines 34 and 35 of his verses the
words “quos certo attingcre pulsu lex jubet,” have been trans-
lated by Perry as “the runner’s bat must touch their pale, or else
the run will nought avail.” His critics have maintained that the
existence of the Popping Crease would render this action super-

THE 1744 AND OTHER EARLY CODES
The earliest version of the Laws which has come down to us is
commonly referred to as the Code of 1744 though no small degree
of unnecessary mystery has been allowed to cloud this issue. It is
such an important milestone that I feel fully justified in putting
on record the somewhat tiresome research which has satisfied me
that the claim is not unfounded.
It is generally assumed by historians that the Laws of Cricket,
if not adopted for the first time, were at least redrafted in 1744;
the further I looked into the matter, the more my doubts grew,
and before giving my verdict it is necessary to collate and com-
ment on the many statements on the subject which some very
eminent men have made from time to time.

As Editor of Scores and Biographies, and the first thorough
historian of the game, it is significant that he ascribes no date to
the two versions of the earliest Laws given in Volume I, and
nowhere does he mention a 1744 code.
Comment: It is certain that Hay gar th would have given a date
if he had been in a position to do so. Nearly every later writer has
focused attention on the first version of the old Laws he quoted,
as having been transcribed from an undated manuscript. Hay-
garth’s second version has passed unnoticed, although in fact, as
will be made clear later, it has every evidence of being the more
authentic, and therefore the more important of the two.

Readers who have borne with me through these tiresome
extractions will appreciate that my doubts about the 1744 Laws
had at least some substance, and will agree that a serious effort to
clear up the uncertainty was justified. Of the eight authorities
from whom I have quoted, and to my knowledge there are no
others, only Ashley-Cooper and Gaston are of any assistance, and
therefore the problem would only be solved if the source of the
former’s version could be established—the clue might well lie in
Gaston’s rather garbled description of his investigations.
Thanks to the kindness of Mr. J. W. Goldman, I have been able
to compare the Laws in The New Universal Magazine of November
1752 with those quoted by Ashley-Cooper in 1900. Apart from
two very minor discrepancies which are probably nothing more
than errors in transcription, they agree word for word, and one
may surmise that Ashley-Cooper followed up the earlier hint
provided by Gaston.
The New Universal Magazine version is described as “The Game
at Cricket, as settled by the cricket-club, in 1744, and play’d at
the artillery-ground, London.” This appears entirely con-
clusive as regards the date, and while the term “settled” does not
necessarily imply that the Laws were printed then, it must surely
mean that some revision at least was carried out in that year. This
result by itself justifies the time spent on my enquiry, but, in
addition, it may also throw light on the dates of other early
versions which have come down to us. In considering this, how-
ever, we must always keep in mind that by 1752 the Laws may
have included changes introduced into the game since 1744, and
they may have been subject to some degree of modernisation.

Conclusions
It is now necessary to formulate tentative conclusions on the
evidence at our disposal, and they are:
First, before matches were played for high stakes as a regular
custom, it is possible that no printed Laws were current
despite the fact that a simple code was commonly accepted
with no doubt some local variations on points of detail.
Second, after cricket had come to London at the beginning of
the eighteenth century, a master code may well have been
maintained at the Artillery Ground, from which copies were
prepared by hand to govern the play in the Grand Matches
played elsewhere.
Third, there is no reason to think that any important changes
occurred during the period 1700 to 1755, and therefore the
code adopted in 1744 was no more than a revision of the
earlier Laws.
Fourth, the Humphry and^Ware handkerchiefs may well have
been the very first printing of the Laws in any shape, just as
the 1752 edition is probably the first to have been type-set.
Having thus discussed at some length all that can relevantly be
learnt of the dark ages, the stage is set to examine the evolution
of the Laws from 1750 to the present time. A detailed chronology
of the actual changes made in the Laws will be found in Chapter
6, but this list of events by its nature will probably have its chief
virtue in its reference value, which in turn will not be fully
realised unless the changing character of the game throughout
the different periods is appreciated.
In order to provide tills general background, the history of the
game can best be divided into periods which fit in with the con-
stitutional aspect of the Laws which governed it. The dividing
lines I have selected are felt to be logical and convenient, since each
period commences with a definite revision of the Laws of the
game. They are:
1744 to 1787 The Hamblcdon Period
1787 to 1835 The Early Marylebone Period
1835 to 1884 The Middle Marylebone Period
Since 1884 The Modern Period
I have sketched the main characteristics of these periods in the
Chapters which follow, and in doing so have drawn attention to

THE SETTING
We have already seen that the stakes and gambling associated
with the more important matches was an essential factor in the
development of cricket, and the hundred years which followed
the revision of the Laws in 1744 provide continuing evidence of
this feature.
It is not out of place, in a book dealing with the Laws of the
game, to mention that Cricket itself was an unlawful pursuit until
after the accession of Queen Victoria, since all games were banned
by an Act of Parhament of the time of Henry VIII (33, c. 9), as
a consequence of “crafty persons having invented new and crafty
games by reason whereof archery is sore decayed.” In 1845, an
amending Act relieved games of skill of their shackles, and only
then did the M.C.C. and Cricket become honest members of
society.
Fortunately neither gambling nor illegitimacy was regarded
as any tiling but normal in the eighteenth century, and the pioneers
quite rightly judged that Cricket should be above the laws of the
land; the application of the Act of Henry VIII was left to the
magistrates, when they were constrained to deal with riotous
gatherings, and to those who, having fairly lost their money on
a match, elected to be sued for their debts rather than to pay as
honourable men. The Act, too, may well account for the lack of
historical records of cricket in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries,
since all games must at least have had a clandestine character.
Whether the Law was generally enforced or not in the early
days may be regarded as doubtful, but in the period with which
we are concerned the very fact that both Royalty and Nobility
became the match-makers provided cricket with the immunity it
deserved. At no other period has Royalty played so active a part
in the game. Frederick, Prince of Wales, was a staunch supporter

Long before 1750 large stakes on matches were usual, and for
the more important ones were as high as 500 and even 1,000
guineas. Such stakes required match-makers, and for the Grand
Matches these were drawn from the ranks of the Bucks of the

Dorset and Richmond, the Earls of Sandwich, March and

Mann became household words.
With such large interests at stake, it was natural that the game
should be attacked on moral grounds—the following extract from

utmost exertion of strength and agility, was followed until of

Cricket matches are now degenerated into business of importance.

There seems to have been a steady tendency for the stakes to
increase up to 1790, and in exceptional cases they rose to 2,000
and even 4,000 guineas. There is no doubt that betting starting
from small private wagers steadily increased, and gradually be-
came more and more professionalised. The coming of the pro-

part of the nineteenth century connected with the selling of matches
by the players, who in some cases were in the hands of the “legs.”
Nyrcn stoutly defended the honesty of his father and the older
generation of Hamblcdon player, but in doing so tacitly admitted
the advent of crooked practices amongst the younger players, and
tliis was later confirmed by William Beldham.
The 1774 Laws for betting were introduced to meet the chang-
ing requirements of the gamblers, and were as follows:
“If the Notches of one Player are laid against another, the Bet
depends on both Innings, unless otherwise specified.
“If one Party beats the other in one Innings, the Notches in the
First Innings shall determine the Bet.
“ But if the other Party goes in a Second Time, then the Bet

From about 1790 the number of games played for high stakes
fell off; probably the number of matches exceeded the supply of
backers; the latter were by then amply cared for by the book-

THE HAMBLEDON PERIOD, 1744 TO 1787
It is true to say that it was during this period that Cricket grew
to man’s estate, and the credit for this is due very largely to the
astonishing prowess of the Hambledon players in what was at
that time a very inaccessible part of Hampshire, particularly for
players living in London and Kent, wliich had hitherto been the
most important centres of the first-class game.
The Hambledon Club was formed about 1750 by, it is believed,
the Reverend Charles Powlett (Paulet or Pawlet), a son of the
3rd Duke of Bolton by Lavinia Fenton who played the original
part of Polly Peachum in Gay’s Beggar s Opera. The formation of
the new club was destined to have a greater impact on the game
than any succeeding event, and within forty years the Hambledon
players lifted cricket from its early immaturity to^the level of a
truly scientific game.
The success of the club was due in part to the enthusiasm of its
THE PRE-VICTORIAN ERA 21
founders which created a wonderful esprit de corps, and in part to
its composition, for in this we see the counterpart of modern
cricket in which amateurs and professionals play alongside each
other in the happiest association to the great benefit of the game.
Its members were drawn mainly from Hampshire, but it also
attracted many of the leading players from London, Kent,
Surrey and Sussex, to form a team whose calibre was undoubted,
and who were also thinking cricketers and great students of the
game.
It was not long before Hambledon could play All England, and,
although they are said to have suffered a temporary set-back
about 1770, between 1772 and 1796 the club won thirty-six out
of seventy-one matches played against England, Kent, Surrey and
Sussex.
The effect of the advance during the period is naturally re-
flected in the changes which were made in the Laws—this effect
is seen in two ways, the type of constitutional authority set up to
deal with the Laws, and the actual changes thereby effected.
It would take time for Hambledon to establish its prestige, and
it is not surprising to find that the 1755 code, published by the
Star and Garter Committee, remained essentially identical with
those settled at the Artillery Ground in 1744. In the next twenty
years, however, the rapid advance of Hambledon both in prestige
and in science demanded changes in the Laws, which, as might be
expected, were mainly directed towards the closer definition
necessary for the more serious game in course of development.
It would seem that the Star and Garter Committee of 1755
was in no sense a standing one—we learn of their Laws being re-
issued in 1767 (ReadingMercury, June 8th), but there is no evidence
that these were more than reprints of the original version: even
as late as 1786 an edition of the 1774 Laws was published con-
taining in addition the 1755 code in its first form, which strongly
suggests that no new Laws were enacted in the intervening years.
This lack of a legislative authority must have proved irksome to
those bent on progress, and in the absence of such a body we have
definite evidence of Hambledon taking the matter into their own
hands, for in 1771 a committee of their players gave an ad hoc
decision to limit the width of the bat.
The story of this change is told by Nyren—his facts being taken
from an account of the early days lent to him by William Ward.

Thus we pass through this all-important period of cricket
history, till at its close we see the transfer of power back from
Hampshire to London on the formation of the M.C.C., and since
the first revision of the Laws carried out by the M.C.C. in 1788
must still reflect the developments pursued by Hambledon, I can
appropriately refer to them here.
The new code contained only four new provisions—of these,
three were closer definitions of the Laws dealing with “L.B.W.,”
the obstruction of a catch, and for the disallowance of runs in the

legislation than had hitherto been provided. The remaining
change provided, for the first time, rules for the rolling, mowing,
etc., of the pitch by mutual agreement during a match—surely
the earliest sign of the now accepted principle that bad conditions

such players as David Harris, of whom Nyren wrote, ‘To Harris’

The period from 1810 to 1835 was concerned almost entirely
with a bitter controversy about the height of the bowler’s arm at
the moment of delivery—in other words whether bowling should
continue to be under-arm or not. The resulting schism must be
described in some detail for two reasons which are relevant to our
main theme: firstly, because it affected the prestige and authority
of the M.C.C. for many years to come, and secondly because the
experience of the period provided many lessons on which the
legislative procedure of later years was founded.
Long previously, Tom Walker of Hambledon had exploited
for a time round-arm, or “throwing” as it was then called, but
it was not until Mr. John Willes of Kent revived this technique
about 1802 that the matter became serious enough for the M.C.C.
to take notice. In effect, for the first time this raised in an acute
form the question, which to-day is perennial, of how to maintain
an even balance between the bowler and the striker. David
Harris by his genius had forced on batsmen a new style of play
and a new shape of bat, which had led to their supremacy on the
field; to it Willes and his imitators thought that illegal, round-arm
bowling was the answer.
This player is said to have owed his methods to practice against
the bowling of his sister, who, it has been suggested, garbed
herself for the occasion in such a voluminous crinoline that she
could not avoid bowling “No balls.” Willes was imitated by
others, so that, in spite of warnings from authority, the practice
of “throwing” was becoming general by 1816; William Ward, a
staunch conservative, then pressed liis views on the M.C.C. to
such effect that they adopted a new version of the “No ball” Law,
which deserves quoting, if only as an example of how not to
frame a law. It read, “The ball must be bowled (not thrown or
jerked), and be delivered underhand, with the hand below the
elbow. But if the ball be jerked, or the arm extended from the
body horizontally, and any part of the back of the hand be upper-
most, or the hand horizontally extended when the ball is de-
livered, the Umpire shall call ‘No ball.’”
This enactment was a tactical blunder which was soon to cause
trouble; the difficulty over the earlier Law was not due to its
wording, but to the weakness of the captains in allowing, and of

Thus ended the first phase of the controversy, but it was not
long before a campaign in favour of the “New style” was opened
on a carefully prepared plan by Mr. George Knight, himself a
member of the M.C.C. A full account of his experimental
matches and of the arguments on both sides is to be found in
the contemporary numbers of The Sporting Magazine of 1827 and
1828, from which it is clear that the case in favour of granting
greater latitude to the bowler was based on the following
grounds:
(i)	Some help to the bowler was necessary as the result of the
great development in batting.
(ii)	The change would not lead to faster bowling or to injuries
to the players, but it would increase the stock of good
bowlers to the advantage of the game, and in particular it
would enable bowlers to impart more spin and lift to the
ball after pitching.

This period falls into two sharply defined phases divided by the
recognition of over-arm bowling in 1864: one theme was common
to both—a constant striving on the part of the bowler to regain
his superiority over the striker, a struggle which naturally had a
direct impact on the legislation of the time.
The latter part of the era saw the start of modern inter-County
cricket, and of the interchange of visits with overseas countries,
both of which were destined later to alter the shape of the Laws—
it also saw the gradual development of the modern perfect pitch,
though this had not reached the stage which required the attention
of authority before the end of the period under review.
Including those made in 1835, there were not more than sixteen
important alterations to the Laws made during the fifty years, and
many of these were of an entirely general nature such as limitations
for the dimensions of the bat and the ball, and the provision of a
“Follow on” rule designed to reduce the number of undecided
matches. The perennial struggle between bowler and batsman
was the chief preoccupation of those responsible for the gover-
nance of the game, and until over-arm bowling was recognised,
the story has a close resemblance to that of the controversy of the
1820’5 in respect of round-arm bowling. Long before 1835 h was
clear that the “New style” bowlers were bowling over-arm
whenever they were allowed to do so by the umpires; thirty years

let Lilly bowl as high as he likes that he beats all that ever did

Special General Meeting of the M.C.C., Lord Harris pointed out

alternative was hardly practicable, he secured a last-minute addi-

1845 formula; this had not been effective under different circum-

resulting problems remained in the hands of the M.C.C., who
having ridden out the storms of the early and middle parts of the

THE 1884 CODE
The year 1884 has been chosen as the start of a new period because
it saw the introduction of a completely revised code of the Laws.
The steps leading up to this are worth recording, since they con-
tained features which were later to prove of much value at the
time the 1947 code was under preparation.
As a result of a discussion on one or two of the Laws at a
meeting of the M.C.C. Committee in February 1883, it was
decided to instruct a sub-committee comprising the Hon. S.
Ponsonby Fane, V. E. Walker, Lord Winterton, Captain W. E.
Denison, C. B. Marsham, the Hon. Alfred Lyttelton, Lord Harris
and the Secretary of the M.C.C., H. Perkins, to revise the Laws
“by defining terms, supplying omissions, and better wording,
but without altering the spirit of the game.” During the follow-
ing summer the draft of the proposed code was circulated for
comment to the County Clubs, the University Clubs, and to the
Cricket Associations of Victoria, New South Wales, Philadelphia
and New York—a circulation showing that the M.C.C. even at
this early time fully appreciated its changing constitutional posi-
tion arising from die rapid spread of the game.
After the replies had been considered, the fmal draft was
adopted at a Special General Meeting of the M.C.C. in April 1884
with only one material amendment—proposed by Lord Harris
and directed towards tightening up the “No ball” Law. This
code—the third revision undertaken by the M.C.C., and destined
to serve for over sixty years—was by far the most adequate ever
produced. As might be expected from the terms of reference
given to the drafting sub-committee no important changes were
made in the intent of the Laws, but their sequence was materially
altered, and much new matter was included to fill gaps previously
left to the imagination; in fact a great deal of tidying up was

STRUCTURAL BACKGROUND
Li no period has there been a more rapid development in the
structure and organisation of cricket, as a result of the increased
facilities for playing, improved means of transport, and the conse-
quent growth of public interest in the game encouraged as it has
been by the Press, Broadcasting, News Reels and Television.
There has been a steady increase in the number of countries
anxious to enter the international arena, accompanied by a
perhaps exaggerated importance being attached to the result of
Test Matches, which to-day test the mental and physical stamina
of the players to an extent unthought of sixty years ago. First-
class intcr-County matches are at least three times as many as in
1884. The Minor Counties’ Championship starting from no tiring
has grown into an important competition in which nearly 150
matches are played in each season. League cricket has gone from
strength to strength, and is now steadily spreading from its place
of birth in the North of England, and clubs without number
have sprung into being all over the world.
Overseas, the period has seen the institution of Inter-State
competitions—in Australia the Sheffield Shield, in South Africa
the Currie Cup, in New Zealand the Plunket Shield, in India the
Ranji Trophy, and in the West Indies the Inter-Colonial Tourna-
ment.
This world-wide development has naturally brought in its
train the administrative machinery necessary for its control, and a
brief account of this building-up process will not be out of place.
In the international field Boards of Control have come into being
in England (1898), Australia (1905), South Africa (1890), New
Zealand, West Indies and India. In 1909, the M.C.C. joined with
Australia and South Africa in establishing the Imperial Cricket

ECONOMIC BACKGROUND
While, similarly, changes in the economic structure of the
country have not materially affected the conduct of the game,
they have in no small measure altered its social shape. Two
Great Wars have profoundly modified every aspect of our
national life, and cricket could not expect to escape the economic
consequences—high taxation, inroads on capital and the break-up
of country estates and other social changes materially added to the
difficulties facing the game which for all practical purposes had
been halted for eleven years between 1914 and 1946.

TECHNICAL BACKGROUND
If, as we have seen, structural and economic factors have hardly
affected the Laws, the technical background of the period most
certainly did so, and one reason was almost entirely responsible—
the marked improvement of the cricket pitches between 1870 and
the end of the century. As late as i860 it is known that only the
pitches at the Oval, Canterbury, Cambridge and Brighton
approached modern standards, and elsewhere the introduction of
over-arm bowling on the rough scythe-cut grounds made the
game a severe trial of nerve. The advent of the mowing-machine,
the heavy roller and Nottingham marl provided the opportunity
for the groundmen; they were not slow to develop their art, and
by 1900 match centres had been transformed.
Every student of the game is agreed that it was the production of
the modern pitch which has changed the technique of the game
in no uncertain manner. It was responsible for the “throwing”
scandal of the last part of the nineteenth century—it enabled
batsmen to follow the example of Arthur Shrewsbury in the
development of back play—it led to the bowlers trying to get
wickets by flight and swerve rather than by movement off the
pitch—it led too to the development of the “googly,” which
resulted in even more attention being paid to playing off the
back foot, and later to the adoption of the exaggerated two-eyed

the batsman’s person outside of the line between wicket and

OTHER MEASURES ADOPTED TO ASSIST THE BOWLER
Individually some of these other measures may appear insignifi-
cant, but collectively they show the degree of thought and care
devoted during the period to the restoration of the balance of
power between the bowler and the batsman.
The first change, made in 1889, permitted a bowler to change
ends as often as he wished in any innings provided he did not
bowl two overs in succession. This change was off-set by another
increasing the over from four to five balls; a further increase to
six balls was made in 1900. These changes were designed to save
time during play, but a trial of the eight-ball over in 1939 with
the same object did not meet with the approval of the players in
England, although the longer over had been a success in Australia
and South Africa for many years.
In 1902 on the recommendation of the County captains the
length of the Bowling crease was increased by two feet to eight
feet eight inches; this change had been proposed by the well-
known umpire Thoms as early as 1888, but subsequent to its
adoption many good judges have held that it was a mistake as
from it sprang an increase in swing bowling and the corre-
sponding partial eclipse of genuine spin bowlers. This view may
have been over-emphasised, as certainly in recent years all too few
bowlers of any type appear to make much use of the crease at all.
The next advance in 1907—and this most certainly did en-
courage seam bowling—was the grant of a new ball after 200
runs had been scored off the old one; in recent years this rule has
been altered experimentally for first-class cricket to one under
which a new ball can be claimed after a fixed number of overs.
In 1925 it came to light that but few of the balls used in first-
class cricket conformed to the Law as regards their weight and
size—particularly the latter. This led at once to the balls being
properly gauged, and next to bitter complaints by the bowlers
that the standard balls were too large. In fact the manufacturers
had in the previous years unwittingly provided an admirable
trial of a smaller ball, which in 1927 was legalised.

“direct attack”
It would be wrong to omit reference to the controversy which
raged in 1933 and 1934 round that type of bowling which came
to be called “bodyline,” and which is still so fresh in the mind of
all cricketers. I do not propose to give the story in full detail as
all who wish to refresh their minds on the subject will find it
described at length in Wisdens Almanack for 1934, 1935 and

A very considerable degree of misunderstanding persists over
the definition of this sort of bowling which has been agreed by all
to “be an offence against the spirit of the game.” In the first
place it is definitely confined to “fast” bowling in its ordinary
sense, and no one has ever suggested that a fast bowler is not per-
fectly entitled to bowl an occasional bouncer, and if he wishes to
do so to place a strong on-side field. These are points which
many followers of the game quite fail to appreciate. Secondly,
the bowler only becomes guilty of an offence if he conducts a
direct attack upon the batsman which in the opinion of the um-
pire is obviously the “persistent and systematic bowling of fast
short-pitched balls at the batsman standing clear of his wicket.”
Before an umpire can decide that an offence against the spirit
of the game is being committed, he must therefore be satisfied on
four points—that the bowling is both fast and short-pitched, that
it is directed against the striker standing clear of his wicket, and
that it is persistent. If he feels that this definition applies in its full
sense, the umpire then knows that an unfair attack is in progress
with which he has the power to deal. The existence or otherwise
of a packed on-side field is only material in as far as it may give
some indication of the motive of the bowler.

had occurred in 1934, that since this type of bowling had been
accepted as being “unfair” the umpires were empowered to deal

action they thought fit to take since the Counties had agreed
“that the County Committees and the County Captains shall take

bowling as now defined, i.e. direct attack.” The instructions
went on to state in explicit terms what action the umpires should
take, and this has now been incorporated as a Note to the Law
dealing with “Unfair Play.”
In considering the manner in which this problem was dealt
with, it should be compared with the story of “throwing”
already given earlier in this chapter. The latter trouble persisted
for years because certain captains decided to depart from the
spirit of the game until the umpires pulled them up. Instead of
dealing with the captains, as was finally done so successfully, the
M.C.C. for too long tried to get the umpires to shoulder an
invidious responsibility. In the case of “direct attack,” this lesson
had been learnt, and the answer to an at least equally difficult
problem was found at once.

well be contrary to the best interest of the game.
LEGISLATION OF A GENERAL NATURE
So far I have only referred to legislation resulting from efforts
to assist the bowlers, and during the period there were but few
others unrelated to this main theme. The only alterations of note
were those concerned with the “follow on” and “declaration”
Laws. Both were primarily directed towards reducing the number
of drawn matches, which resulted from the huge scores following
on the improvement of pitches and batting technique.
It has always been something of a mystery that, while the
“follow on” Law dates from 1835, “declarations” were not
provided for until 1889—this is the more extraordinary since the
inspiration for both can be traced to the desire to avoid the
burlesque of batsmen knocking down their own wickets, or
deliberately getting themselves out in other ways.
The “follow on” Law was the subject of much muddled
thought during the latter part of the nineteenth century when it
was still obligatory. The University matches of 1891, 1893 and
1896 all pointed to the absurdity of a rule which forced a side to
field for two consecutive innings on the improved pitches of the
day, and then possibly to have to bat last on a crumbling pitch.
It required however considerable pressure from Australia, and
the failure to win a Test match after Australia had automatically
followed on in 1899 at Old Trafford, to bring about the change
to an optional rule in the following year.
CONSTITUTIONAL AND OTHER DEVELOPMENTS
Owing to the increasing importance of highly competitive
cricket it gradually became necessary for the M.C.C. to supple-
ment the Laws of Cricket in various ways. The pronouncement
on the “L.B.W.” problem in 1888 was followed by others which
have led to the introduction of interpretations of a case law nature,
and to the issue of what to-day are termed “Instructions to
Umpires.”

Wc have seen that proposals to alter the Laws themselves
required the formal approval of a Special General Meeting of the
M.C.C., and in 1894 a new Rule to govern this procedure was
introduced in the following terms: “The Laws of Cricket shall
not at any time be changed, except by a vote of two-thirds of
Members present and voting at a Special General Meeting, of
which notice shall be given in The Times and two Sporting papers,
not less than two months before the day of the Meeting. Any
amendment to a proposed alteration shall be advertised not less
than one month before the day of Meeting. This notice shall state
in distinct terms, the change in the law to be proposed, and no
change shall be declared carried unless twenty Members at least
shall vote in favour of it.”
This formula clearly aimed at avoiding hasty interference with
the conduct of the game, and its effect was felt both in 1901,
when a proposal to alter the “L.B.W.” Law received a majority
vote insufficient for its passage into law, and again in 1903 when
a resolution to add one inch to the width of the wicket met
with a similar fate.
When it is remembered that no Law, or an amendment to an
existing one, is ever submitted for adoption without prior con-
sultation with Governing Bodies Overseas, and that every shade
of opinion in the United Kingdom is taken into account, there
can be little danger of the spirit and character of the game
changing. Before any major change is accepted, it is usual to
try it out experimentally for one or two seasons, as is being done
at the present time in connection with the provision of a new ball
during the progress of an innings.
As early as 1888 a proposal submitted by the M.C.C. Committee
to even the balance between bowler and batsman was referred
back by the Annual Meeting on the ground that there had not
been sufficient time for its examination, and in particular that the
Counties and Overseas Bodies had not been consulted. This
provides an early and interesting example of the care exercised over
any point which might affect the general well-being of the game.
It is interesting to recall that, when resubmitted in the following
year, the proposal was completely accepted which suggests that
the reference back was made solely on constitutional grounds,
and in no way to obstruct the passage of a useful piece of legisla-
tion.

Chapter Five
THE PREPARATION OF THE 1947 CODE
When a Bill is presented to Parliament to become the law of the
land after passing through its several readings in both Houses,
the procedure is well established, and understood by the elec-
torate. In the case of a new code to govern the game of Cricket,
the M.C.C. in 1944 had no precedent later than 1884 to which
they could refer, and their dependency spanned the globe. A
Parliamentary Bill is drafted by lawyers in a form designed to
produce the minimum of litigation in its later application; its
language is therefore that of the lawyer, and more often than not
much of it is obscure to the layman. In addition, however con-
tentious the Bill, given a working majority in Parliament the
Government can be sure of its passage into law without damaging
amendment.
The Laws of Cricket cannot and must not, be couched in strict
legal terms, since they are drafted by laymen for use by laymen—
in many cases by men of humble education, or even with little
knowledge of the English language. Furthermore, as many will
agree, in such a revision it is never desirable to jettison picturesque
phrases of the eighteenth-century tradition, unless they arc mis-
leading in their intent. Finally, whereas an Act of Parhament
aims at being all-embracing, it has always been the glory of
Cricket that its Laws do not set out to cover every small point in
a complicated game, but leave much unwritten for sensible inter-
pretation by players imbued with its spirit.
Acts of Parliament, despite the greatest care of their expert
draftsmen, normally prove to have untidy ends, and it is not to be
expected that a new code of the Laws of Cricket can avoid
obscurities.
The formal acceptance of such a new code depends in the first
place on its receiving a two-thirds majority at a Special General
Meeting of the M.C.C., on which occasion the Committee have
no assured party backing marshalled by the party wliips. Its

Just as Acts of Parliament are supplemented by case law de-

official interpretations, and in most cases the quicker these can be
provided the better it will be for the prestige of the responsible
authority and for the future of the game. In Cricket, whereas an
alteration to the Laws requires the approval of a Special General
Meeting of the M.C.C., the committee of the Club have never
since its formation failed to enquire into any matter brought to
their notice, and to issue, whenever necessary, an interpretation

umpires, or as Notes added to the Laws.
We have seen that the adoption of the 1884 revision synchro-

The late Sir Stanley Jackson had long previously suggested the
necessity for a complete overhaul of the code to Lacey, whose
reply invariably was, “You make the attempt, and then if you see
daylight, I’ll join you in the task.” Lacey, of course, appreciated
the dangers and difficulties attendant on any tampering with an
already accepted code of procedure; it could only succeed if all
the wide-flung interests were drawn into consultation with the
strictly limited objective of collating and tidying up the existing
code, resisting entirely all temptation to make changes in the
conduct of the game, for which inevitably conflicting suggestions
would be received. This, we have already seen, was the basis of
the 1884 revision, and in my opinion must always apply in similar
circumstances if the task of revision is ever to be finished without
the introduction of objectionable compromises, which are inappro-
priate for any game, and may well lead to loss of prestige in the
position of the governing authority.
Before the end of the Second World War, the 1884 code had
been in use for over sixty years, and clearly the psychological
moment to obtain agreement on a successor would be before
cricket once more resumed the even tenor of its way amidst the
inevitable chaos of the peace to come; Sir Stanley Jackson then
in 1944 obtained the agreement of the M.C.C. Committee to a
thorough revision.
Apart from the desirability of collating the Laws and their
Notes, the 1884 code, thorough as its revision had been, disclosed
one or two glaring omissions, and the sequence of its Laws was
by no means logical. The amendments during its long life had in
all cases not been too happily worded, and in any case amend-

elasticity to meet the requirements of the various levels of com-
petitive cricket, or of the differing climates in which the game had
come to be played.

Note to Serial Numbers 8 and 9
Prior to 1700 the form of the wicket is a matter of some doubt, and it seems likely that
it varied from district to district. Nyren, quoting from a MS. lent him by William Ward,
has described one pattern which is of particular interest as suggesting the origin of the
term “Popping.” He records that, “Between the stumps a hole was cut in the ground,
large enough to contain the ball and the butt-end of the bat. In running a notch, the
striker was required to put his bat into this hole instead of the modem practice of touching
over the popping crease. The wicket-keeper, in putting our die striker when running,
was obliged, when the ball was thrown in. to place it in this hole before the adversary
could reach it with his bat. Many severe injuries of the hands were the consequence of
this regulation; the present mode of touching the popping crease was therefore substi-
tuted for it.” In other words the batsman to be home had to pop his bat into the popping
hole before the wicket-keeper popped the ball into it.
Haygarth and Pycrofc lend support to the existence of this form of wicket, and two oil
paintings in the M.C C. Collection actually show it in use. These paintings may not be
strictly contemporary, but they arc early enough to provide supporting evidence of the
true origin of the term “Popping.” Haygarth’s suggestion that this form of wicket may
have remained in use until the late eighteenth century docs not, however, appear to have
any substance.
Goldwin’s description of cricket in 1706. supported as it is by the Richmond Articles
of Agreement of 1727 (vide Chapter 1), is evidence of the disappearance of the Popping
Hole at a considerably earlier date The completion of a run by “touching the Umpire’s
stick” appears to have been the immediate forerunner of the Popping Crease at least as
a local variation in parts of Sussex and Surrey The general introduction of the crease
could hardly have been long delayed as we can imagine in the case of a run out that an
umpire in danger of being run down by the batsman would have found it difficult to
concentrate his attention on the breaking of the wicket.
